# A Better Question for a Public Decision

Imagine a resident hearing that a bill could strengthen bridges and help small businesses during construction. The resident wants to know which communities would benefit, what the program might cost, and whether an amendment could answer concerns about oversight.

Civic Accord offers a place to practice those questions. Today it is a **prototype built from fictional records**. Its sample bill, districts, sources, comments, and scores are not official evidence. The point of the exercise is to make a decision process inspectable.

## Begin with what the bill says

The resident opens the Community Resilience and Enterprise Act in the Bills workspace. Three short sections describe an infrastructure guarantee, a continuity credit, and a reporting requirement. A budget figure appears beside each summary. Source cards show the kind of context someone might want to examine.

That is the first useful pause: the resident can distinguish the proposal's different parts. The cards, however, contain sample excerpts. To discuss an actual bill, the resident would need its current statutory text and attributable primary sources.

## Ask what changes with place

In Impact, the resident chooses one of three sample district profiles. The app combines stored section and district values to calculate an exposure equivalent. Changing the district changes the number.

The number shows how an assumption works. It is not a measured outcome, a forecast, or a literal percentage of residents who will benefit. The displayed confidence also comes from stored inputs; it has not been calibrated against real-world results. The useful question is therefore: *Which assumptions drove this difference, and what evidence would we need before relying on it?*

## Make priorities visible

In Voter feedback, a preset group's issue sliders show how quadratic token costs work. One vote costs one token; three votes cost nine. The page displays a 50-token budget, so putting more emphasis on one issue makes a visible trade-off.

No public vote occurs. The page allows overspending, saves nothing, and sends no choice to a representative. Its value is to let a reader test whether this form of priority setting is understandable and fair enough to study further.

## Compare options without hiding the assumptions

Compromise analysis shows four preset amendments. Each has assumed utility values for three groups, plus risk and complexity values. The app calculates an average, shows the lowest group value, and applies a risk penalty. It highlights options that are not dominated on these measures.

One option appears as the recommendation because it has the highest risk-adjusted score. That is a statement about the sample numbers, not a conclusion that the option is legal, acceptable to affected people, or best for the country. The group's stated hard boundaries are displayed but not enforced by the scoring code. A reader can still use the comparison to ask where support is weak and which assumptions deserve challenge.

## Keep judgment with people

The Trust workspace displays scores for sample participants. Those scores come from four stored attributes and do not alter the amendment ranking. They are an invitation to ask how credibility should be established and corrected, not a measure of anyone's civic worth.

A real decision needs more: authoritative documents, measurable outcomes, public participation safeguards, independent review, and a way to correct mistakes. It needs people who can explain why a choice was made and remain accountable for what follows.

Civic Accord's current contribution is small and concrete. It makes a possible sequence of questions visible: **What is proposed? What is assumed? Who may be affected? Which trade-offs are being compared? What remains unknown?** That sequence can help a conversation become more careful, even while the answers still require work beyond the app.
