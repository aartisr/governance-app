Civic Accord: proposal notes and present implementation

Civic Accord explores a way to make legislative discussion more transparent: show what a proposal says, how assumptions shape local estimates, how strongly groups prioritize issues, and how preset amendments compare under stated objectives. This is a design proposal, not a claim that the prototype has improved policy outcomes.

What the prototype does today

- It displays three fictional bills, three fictional district profiles, sample source excerpts, sample comments, and four preset amendments from local data.
- It calculates quadratic costs for page-local priority sliders. A displayed 50-token budget is not enforced and choices are neither saved nor submitted.
- It calculates illustrative district exposure equivalents from stored section and district values. These are not forecasts or estimates of causal impact.
- It calculates sample participant trust scores from four stored attributes. Those scores do not affect amendment rankings.
- It compares preset amendments using assumed group utilities, risk, and complexity. The Pareto frontier identifies options that are not dominated on three calculated scores. Displayed group boundaries are not enforced.

What a future civic system would require

A real deployment would require authoritative bill text, traceable source documents, validated impact methods, participation safeguards, explicit constraints, public correction processes, privacy and accessibility review, and outcome evaluation. No mathematical score can by itself establish public consent or settle legal and ethical questions.

Research questions

1. Can an intensity-aware feedback design reveal useful priorities without excluding less-resourced people or enabling coercion?
2. Can local impact estimates be calibrated, explained, and independently audited?
3. Which constraints must be treated as binding before amendment options are compared?
4. Do transparent multi-objective comparisons improve understanding or deliberation in a controlled evaluation?
5. How would prediction errors and affected communities' feedback be incorporated without amplifying past bias?

A useful evaluation would pre-register outcomes, compare the proposed workflow with an appropriate baseline, publish assumptions and failures, and avoid claiming success from a toy example alone.
