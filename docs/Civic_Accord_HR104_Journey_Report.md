## The decision in one minute

The fictional **Community Resilience and Enterprise Act (HR-104)** combines infrastructure bond guarantees, small-business continuity credits, and district-level public reporting. Civic Accord helps a visitor inspect those parts, see how assumed local inputs change illustrative exposure, try a limited voice-token exercise, and compare four proposed amendments. Under equal stakeholder weights, the **five-year sunset with renewal vote** has the highest displayed risk-adjusted score (**0.642**). The result is a starting point for questions, **not a vote, policy forecast, or real-world recommendation**.

## The journey

| Step | Visitor question | What the prototype shows |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1. Read the bill | What would change? | Three sample sections, assumed budget changes, and example evidence cards. |
| 2. See local impact | Who might be affected here? | District-adjusted exposure equivalents and the assumptions behind them. |
| 3. Set priorities | What matters most? | A practice budget of 50 voice tokens; priority points cost their square. |
| 4. Compare options | Is there an amendment all groups can discuss? | Four utility vectors, Pareto status, and a separate risk-adjusted ranking. |
| 5. Inspect trust | What supports the displayed participant scores? | Four assumed trust inputs, shown separately from the amendment ranking. |
| 6. Review the brief | What did we learn, and what next? | The modeled leader, alternative options, a sensitivity reversal, and questions for discussion. |

## What we learned

1. **A bill is more than one score.** The proposed bond, credit, and reporting provisions should be examined separately. The evidence excerpts in this prototype are examples, not verified primary documents.
2. **Place changes the example.** District profiles alter the displayed exposure equivalents. For the Bay Civic District, the infrastructure section shows **33.3%** illustrative exposure under the stored assumptions; this is not an observed impact.
3. **Intensity has a cost.** In the practice exercise, three priority points spend nine of 50 tokens. These allocations are not saved, counted as votes, or fed into the compromise score.
4. **The amendment choice depends on judgment.** All four sample amendments are Pareto non-dominated. The ranking therefore requires additional, declared weights and penalties. Trust scores and local impact estimates also remain separate from that ranking.

## Four amendment options

| Amendment | What it changes | Equal-weight modeled score |
|:--|:--|--:|
| Five-year sunset with renewal vote | Requires renewal tied to impact evidence and district reporting. | **0.642** |
| Transparent need-weighted formula | Publishes need, vulnerability, and readiness weights with a readable rationale. | 0.581 |
| Automatic audit trigger | Requires independent audits after specified spending or impact misses. | 0.562 |
| Targeted continuity credit | Narrows credits to affected corridors and adds a rural carveout. | 0.527 |

**What changes the answer?** With Growth Caucus at 0.2×, Equity Bloc at 2.5×, and Fiscal Guardians at 1.0×, the transparent need-weighted formula becomes the modeled leader. The result changes because the weights changed; no new evidence was collected.

## What to do next

- **Check the proposal.** Revisit each section. Before any real policy discussion, obtain current primary bill text and independently verify every source.
- **Question the local model.** Ask how district inputs were chosen, whose circumstances are missing, and whether alternative assumptions reverse the exposure estimates.
- **Discuss the trade-offs.** Ask who gains, who bears costs, which boundaries are non-negotiable, and who should set the weights. Use the four amendments as discussion options, not an automated decision.

## Boundary of this report

This report describes the **working interactive website's fictional HR-104 sample** at its baseline settings. It does not capture an individual visitor's clicks, district selection, voice-token allocation, or slider changes. It does not submit feedback, verify evidence, contact officials, establish policy effects, or evaluate real people. A full civic decision system would require verified data, community governance, privacy protection, independent validation, and funding. For the implemented mathematics and citations, see Ravikumar, *From Voice to Verifiable Compromise: An Inspectable Mathematics for Civic Decision Support* (2026), available on the Civic Accord homepage.
